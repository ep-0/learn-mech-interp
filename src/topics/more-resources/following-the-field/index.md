## What This List Is Selected For

Interpretability moves fast enough that a curriculum is always slightly behind, and the gap is filled by conversations rather than papers. This page is the passive half of that: things worth listening to on a walk, watching when you are too tired to read, or keeping in a feed.

Everything here shares one property. The people making it are accountable to an audience that would notice if they were wrong, and they behave accordingly: they hedge where the evidence is thin, they interview people who disagree with them, and they do not manufacture stakes. That excludes most AI content, which is optimized for a viewer who wants to feel informed in eight minutes.

> **The filter used here:** would a researcher in the field be comfortable being interviewed on it, and comfortable being quoted from it afterwards? Almost everything sensational fails that test, because sensational framing is exactly what makes a careful person decline.

## Podcasts

**[AXRP: the AI X-risk Research Podcast](https://axrp.net/)** is the closest thing the field has to a technical journal you can listen to. Daniel Filan interviews alignment and interpretability researchers for two or three hours about one piece of work, and he has read it properly. The episodes with interpretability researchers are the single best audio resource for this curriculum. Start with an episode on a paper you have already read; the contrast between the paper and the author's unguarded account of it is the point.

**[The 80,000 Hours Podcast](https://80000hours.org/podcast/)** does long-form interviews across AI safety, economics, and policy. The AI episodes are technically serious without assuming you work in the field, and the interviewer's habit of pushing back on his own guests is rarer than it should be.

**[The Inside View](https://theinsideview.ai/)** is Michaël Trazzi interviewing researchers, often the ones whose papers appear in this curriculum, at length and without a script.

**[Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkeshpatel.com/)** interviews AI researchers alongside historians and economists. Unusually well prepared, and willing to spend an hour on one disagreement. The breadth is the appeal: it is the best argument on this list that AI is not a self-contained subject.

**[Machine Learning Street Talk](https://www.youtube.com/@MachineLearningStreetTalk)** is the most technical of these and the least edited. The hosts argue with guests about foundations, which makes it uneven and occasionally excellent.

## Video

**[3Blue1Brown](https://www.3blue1brown.com/)** you will already be using for the mathematics. The neural network and transformer series are worth watching even after you can derive the content, because the visual intuitions transfer to how you explain your own work.

**[Andrej Karpathy](https://www.youtube.com/@AndrejKarpathy)** builds things from nothing, in real time, without skipping the parts that are hard. *Let's build GPT* and the tokenizer video are prerequisites for several lessons in the plan; the rest of the channel is worth the time regardless.

**[Neel Nanda](https://www.youtube.com/@neelnanda2469)** posts paper walkthroughs and unedited research sessions. The recorded exploratory analyses are the closest thing available to watching an interpretability researcher think, including the dead ends that papers never show.

**[Welch Labs](https://www.youtube.com/@WelchLabsVideo)** makes carefully researched, beautifully made videos on machine learning history and mathematics. Slow in the best sense.

**[Artem Kirsanov](https://www.youtube.com/@ArtemKirsanov)** covers computational neuroscience and its overlap with machine learning. Useful for this curriculum specifically, since manifold and population-coding ideas arrived from that direction.

**[Mutual Information](https://www.youtube.com/@Mutual_Information)** does statistics and probability with unusual care about what a method assumes.

{% sidenote "Paper-review channels are the category where quality varies most. Yannic Kilcher's reviews are technically solid and worth watching once you can already judge a paper, because his opinions are strong and stated as opinions. Treat a review as a starting point rather than as a verdict." %}

## Writing Worth Following

**[Transformer Circuits Thread](https://transformer-circuits.pub/)** publishes most of the interpretability results this curriculum covers, in a format that shows the working. Reading a new post as it appears, before anyone has summarized it, is good practice.

**[AI Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/)** and the interpretability tag on LessWrong are where results are argued about before and after publication. The comment sections are frequently more informative than the posts.

**[Distill](https://distill.pub/)** stopped publishing, and its archive is still the best example of what explanation can be when someone takes the medium seriously.

**[Anthropic Alignment Science](https://alignment.anthropic.com/)** and **[Neel Nanda's blog](https://www.neelnanda.io/)** post shorter, faster work: negative results, methodological notes, and advice that does not fit in a paper.

**[Import AI](https://importai.substack.com/)** by Jack Clark is a weekly summary with judgment attached, from someone who has to be right about the field for a living. **[Ahead of AI](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/)** by Sebastian Raschka covers technical developments with implementations. **[Interconnects](https://www.interconnects.ai/)** by Nathan Lambert is the best current writing on post-training, which is the stage most safety-relevant behavior comes from.

## Books

Read these for perspective rather than for technique. None will help with an experiment next week, and all of them will make you better at choosing what to work on.

**Brian Christian, *The Alignment Problem*** is the best popular account of why this field exists, and it is accurate enough that researchers cite it. **Stuart Russell, *Human Compatible*** makes the argument from the other direction, starting from what an objective is.

**Judea Pearl, *The Book of Why*** you will meet in the causal inference block; read it as a book rather than as a reference, because the argument builds. **Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans*** is the most useful skeptical treatment, and taking its objections seriously is more productive than dismissing them.

**Richard Hamming, *The Art of Doing Science and Engineering*** is not about AI at all. It is about choosing important problems, and it is the book most likely to change how you spend the next decade.

<details class="pause-and-think">
<summary>Pause and think: what is this actually for?</summary>

Passive consumption feels like learning and mostly is not, which is the same trap the [learning plan](/plan/) is built to avoid. Given that, what is a podcast episode about a paper you have read actually good for?

The useful answer is calibration rather than content. An author talking unguardedly about their own work will tell you which results they believe, which they think are fragile, and what they would do differently. None of that survives peer review, and all of it is what you need in order to judge the literature rather than absorb it. If you are listening to learn the content, read the paper instead.

</details>

## How to Use This Without Drowning

Pick two, not ten. A feed you actually read beats a folder of subscriptions you feel guilty about, and the field produces far more than anyone can follow.

Consumption is not study. The [learning plan](/plan/) allocates hours to reading, retrieval, and practice, and none of them are hours spent listening to someone else think. Treat this page as what fills the time the plan does not.
